## 3 Further Details

Basis for the Standards:

The numerical ranges for the indicators in this test are our own assumptions. We referred to the Royal College of Physicians' NEWS2 clinical indicators and relevant literature from NASA's Apollo program when selecting indicators and observing multiple vital signs together. The ranges in this table were set by the team for prototype testing.

Reasons for Selecting the Indicators:

Heart rhythm (HR): Low gravity and other spaceflight stress factors can change the heart and blood vessels, potentially leading to blood clots, arrhythmias, cardiovascular disease, and low blood pressure, thereby increasing the likelihood of fainting upon landing.

Oxygen saturation (SpO₂): During flight, a rapid decrease in ambient pressure may cause a condition resembling decompression sickness. If the reading stays low or drops rapidly, the system can prompt a repeat measurement and a check of symptoms and the cabin oxygen environment to help identify possible hypoxia early.

Respiratory rate (RR): Breathing that is too fast, too slow, or persistently changing can indicate that a crew member's respiratory status needs immediate confirmation. Considering RR together with SpO₂, heart rate, and symptoms provides more information for respiratory alerts.

Systolic blood pressure (SBP)/diastolic blood pressure (DBP): Blood pressure serves as a reference indicator for heart rhythm. During flight, blood shifts upward due to gravity, causing facial congestion and swelling and nasal congestion in astronauts, while the legs become thinner. This is called Puffy face-bird leg syndrome and may increase intracranial pressure, causing headaches.

Body temperature (°C): An elevated body temperature is considered a sign of an acute physiological illness even on Earth. It can affect work performance and consciousness, and a still higher temperature can be life-threatening. Recent research indicates that body temperature may rise in microgravity and may rise faster during exercise.

Environmental radiation rate/personal cumulative dose: The radiation dose astronauts are exposed to during missions is much higher than on Earth. Long-term exposure may increase effects on tissues including the heart, blood vessels, and eyes. Radiation in space may also affect the central nervous system, causing cognitive or executive-function difficulties during a mission and possibly increasing astronauts' cancer risk.
